Quantile Mapping for Climate Downscaling

Five papers that define the method, its failure modes, and the two repairs that keep a projected trend intact. Read alongside notebook 03.

Five papers

What quantile mapping is, and which variant actually wins

Gudmundsson, L., Bremnes, J. B., Haugen, J. E., Engen-Skaugen, T. (2012). Technical Note: Downscaling RCM precipitation to the station scale using statistical transformations — a comparison of methods. Hydrol. Earth Syst. Sci. 16, 3383–3390. doi:10.5194/hess-16-3383-2012 · CC BY 3.0
Gudmundsson2012_QM_transformations_HESS.pdf

Why it matters here

This is the definitional paper. It states quantile mapping as a probability integral transform, sorts every published variant into three families, and then ranks them on real data with cross-validation. It also gives our project its vocabulary: the authors list eight names for the same method (“quantile mapping”, “CDF matching”, “distribution mapping”, …) and settle on statistical transformation. The companion R package qmap comes from this paper.

Core method

Find a function h that maps modelled values so their distribution matches the observed one:

Po = h(Pm) Eq. 1 — the general statement. Deterministic: one modelled value always gives one corrected value.
Po = Fo−1(Fm(Pm)) Eq. 2 — the working form. Push the modelled value through the modelled CDF to get its non-exceedance probability, then read that probability off the observed quantile function. This single line is quantile mapping.

The three families differ only in how h is approximated:

  • Distribution derived — assume theoretical CDFs (Bernoulli–Gamma, Bernoulli–Weibull, Bernoulli–Lognormal) and fit them by maximum likelihood.
  • Parametric — fit the q–q relation directly, e.g. P̂o = bPm (simple scaling), a + bPm, or b(Pm − x)c.
  • Nonparametric — use the empirical CDFs. The paper assesses two: QUANT tabulates empirical percentiles and interpolates linearly between them, carrying the highest quantile's correction for values beyond the training range (after Boé et al. 2007); SSPLIN fits a cubic smoothing spline through the q–q cloud, with the smoothing parameter chosen by generalised cross-validation. Note: RQUANT, often cited alongside these, is not in this paper — it is a third nonparametric option in the authors' qmap package, and should be cited to the package, not to the 2012 article.

Key numbers

82Norwegian precipitation stations, 1960–2000
25 kmHIRHAM RCM, ERA-40 driven
2Best methods, both nonparametric: SSPLINE and QUANT

Skill is scored as mean absolute error on the full distribution and on each decile (MAE0.1 … MAE1.0), all under cross-validation. Nonparametric transformations win across the whole range, including the extreme percentiles. Parametric forms with three or more free parameters come close; simple scaling does not.

“these techniques should not be applied without checking their suitability for the data under consideration” Gudmundsson et al. 2012, Conclusions

Caveats

Flexibility cuts both ways. The authors warn that nonparametric fits can overfit when calibrated on short records, and that skill is not guaranteed if the projected climate differs substantially from the calibration period. Both warnings apply directly to our 14d window scheme, where each window holds only 14 values.

Why quantile mapping cannot downscale

Maraun, D. (2013). Bias Correction, Quantile Mapping, and Downscaling: Revisiting the Inflation Issue. J. Climate 26, 2137–2143. doi:10.1175/JCLI-D-12-00821.1
Maraun2013_QM_InflationIssue_JClimate.pdf

Why it matters here

This is the paper that questions the whole premise of our baseline, and it is the most important one to read carefully. Maraun separates two jobs people ask quantile mapping to do at once: correcting a bias (model and observations at the same resolution) and downscaling (observations much finer than the model). The first is defensible. The second, he argues, is not — it is the old variance inflation mistake in new clothes. Our setup is exactly the second case: CMIP6 at ~1° mapped onto ERA5-Land at 0.1°, roughly a hundred fine pixels per coarse cell.

The argument

Quantile mapping adjusts only the marginal distribution at each location. It adds no new small-scale variability. So if you map a grid-box time series onto point data:

  • The temporal structure of the corrected series is still the grid box's, not the local scale's.
  • The spatial dependence between locations inside one grid box becomes fully deterministic — every point in the cell is a fixed function of the same input.
  • Area-mean extremes are overestimated and the drizzle effect is overcorrected, because per-point corrected series are far too coherent when averaged back up.
  • Trends are altered, so projected changes in means and extremes are misrepresented.

The mechanism is von Storch's (1999) inflation critique: rescaling a series to match observed variance wrongly assumes the predictor explains all of the local variance. The remedy there was randomization; the remedy here is a stochastic bias correction.

One sentence that matters for us

“The effect might be less important for temperature, as this variable has a much higher spatial coherence” Maraun 2013, Conclusions

He adds that small-scale temperature variation mostly comes from orographic effects, which he classes as correctable. That is a direct licence for our approach — a temperature predictand, plus an explicit elevation term — but it is a caveat, not an endorsement, and it should be quoted as such.

Setup

25 kmREMO RCM, one grid box, Harz Mountains
20Rain gauges inside that single box
1961–2000ERA-40 driven, no separate validation period

Trends are tested twice: seasonal totals by linear regression, and seasonal maxima by a GEV with time-dependent location and scale, yi ~ GEV(μi, σi, ξ), with μi = aμ + bμti.

Caveats

One grid box, one mountain region, precipitation only. Maraun notes he repeated the analysis in flat terrain and with a second RCM with the same result, but the paper itself is a targeted demonstration, not a survey.

Note his warning about spatial averaging: averaging neighbouring grid boxes to raise the signal-to-noise ratio widens the scale gap and makes inflation worse.

Quantile mapping corrupts projected trends

Cannon, A. J., Sobie, S. R., Murdock, T. Q. (2015). Bias Correction of GCM Precipitation by Quantile Mapping: How Well Do Methods Preserve Changes in Quantiles and Extremes? J. Climate 28, 6938–6959. doi:10.1175/JCLI-D-14-00754.1
Cannon2015_QDM_JClimate.pdf

Why it matters here

The standard reference for quantile delta mapping (QDM), and the clearest statement of the trend problem. Plain QM builds its transfer function from the historical period alone and ignores the future projection entirely. Apply it to a warming world and the model's own climate change signal comes out distorted — sometimes wildly. Since our future scope runs to 2081–2100, this decides whether the baseline we build can be used for projections at all.

Three algorithms, in order

x̂m,p(t) = Fo,h−1{Fm,h[xm,p(t)]} Eq. 1 — QM. Subscripts: o = observed, m = modelled, h = historical, p = projected. With empirical CDFs this is a lookup table interpolated from the historical q–q plot, and it is only defined over the historical modelled range — anything beyond needs extrapolation.
x̂m,p(t) = Fo,h−1{Fm,h[x̄m,h · xm,p(t) / x̄m,p(t)]} · x̄m,p(t) / x̄m,h Eq. 2 — DQM. Divide out the modelled trend in the long-term mean, quantile map, then multiply it back. Preserves the trend in the mean, but not necessarily in the tails. For an interval variable such as temperature, do this additively rather than multiplicatively.

QDM does the same thing quantile by quantile. Start from the non-exceedance probability of the projected value in its own period:

τm,p(t) = Fm,p(t)[xm,p(t)] Eq. 3 — estimated from a time window around t.
Δm(t) = xm,p(t) / Fm,h−1[τm,p(t)] Eq. 4 — the model's own relative change at that quantile. Worked example from the paper: a 2065 value of 36.5 sits at the 99th percentile of the 2050s; the same percentile in the historical run is 28.5; so Δ = 36.5 / 28.5 = 1.28.
x̂m,p(t) = Fo,h−1[τm,p(t)] · Δm(t) Eqs. 5–6 — bias correct that quantile against observations, then re-apply the model's relative change on top. In the example: 22.6 × 1.28. Relative changes in every quantile are preserved by construction.

Key numbers

Applied to CMIP5 daily precipitation over Canada, scored on ETCCDI extremes indices and a GEV analysis of annual maxima. Projected relative change in the 20-year return value by the 2080s, at the worst locations:

+120%Raw GCM — never exceeds this
+140%QDM maximum
+240%DQM maximum
>+1500%QM — inflated far past the model signal

Ranking on the extremes indices is consistent: QDM best, DQM close behind, QM “outperformed by a large margin”. The damage is worst for long-period return values.

Caveats

Every method here still assumes bias stationarity — that the historical error structure carries into the future. Cannon et al. explicitly place that question out of scope and point to Maraun (2012) and Maraun et al. (2010).

QDM is not guaranteed to preserve changes in the mean, only in the quantiles; DQM is the reverse. And the paper is about precipitation, so the multiplicative forms need switching to additive for temperature.

Scaled distribution mapping, with an explicit temperature recipe

Switanek, M. B., Troch, P. A., Castro, C. L., Leuprecht, A., Chang, H.-I., Mukherjee, R., Demaria, E. M. C. (2017). Scaled distribution mapping: a bias correction method that preserves raw climate model projected changes. Hydrol. Earth Syst. Sci. 21, 2649–2666. doi:10.5194/hess-21-2649-2017 · CC BY 3.0
Switanek2017_ScaledDistributionMapping_HESS.pdf

Why it matters here

Two things make this the most directly usable paper of the five. First, it attacks the assumption underneath plain QM head-on: the correction function is not time-invariant, so QM has no justification for inflating or deflating any moment of the climate change signal. Second — unlike Cannon et al. — it writes out a separate, step-by-step algorithm for temperature, fitted with a normal distribution. That is our variable.

The temperature algorithm, step by step

  1. Detrend the raw modelled and observed series (a linear trend is used). Trends are added back at the very end.
  2. Fit a normal distribution to the detrended observations, the raw historical model, and the raw future model — so the parameters are just the empirical mean and standard deviation. Clip CDF values to e.g. [0.0001, 0.9999], because rounding to exactly 0 or 1 gives an infinite temperature.
  3. Scale the fitted future distribution against the fitted historical one, at the probability of each future event.
  4. Recurrence intervals for all three sorted arrays.
  5. Rescale those recurrence intervals and convert back to CDF values.
  6. Bias correct, then reinsert values at their original dates and add the modelled trend back in.
SFA = [ICDFMODF(CDFMODF) − ICDFMODH(CDFMODF)] × (σOBS / σMODH) Eq. 8 — the array of absolute scaling factors (absolute, not multiplicative, because temperature is an interval variable). The variance ratio σOBS/σMODH rescales the model's signal to the observed spread.
RI = 1 / (0.5 − |CDF − 0.5|) Eq. 9 — recurrence interval. Note this differs from the precipitation version (their Eq. 4) to reflect the two-tailed normal distribution.
CDFSCALED = 0.5 + sgn(CDFOBS − 0.5) · |0.5 − 1/RISCALED| Eq. 10 — map the rescaled recurrence interval back onto a probability, keeping the correct tail.
BCINITIAL = ICDFOBS(CDFSCALED) + SFA Eq. 11 — the corrected value: read the observed quantile function at the rescaled probability, then add the absolute scaling factor.

Behaviour worth knowing

  • Where the historical model period and the correction period fully overlap, SDM returns the observed distribution exactly — except that the trend is the model's, not the observations'. That makes it a clean sanity check.
  • SDM outperforms QM, QDM, and DQM at preserving the raw model's projected changes for both temperature and precipitation.
  • Precipitation and temperature differ in three places: multiplicative vs. absolute scaling, wet-day subsetting vs. all values, and detrending (temperature only).

Caveats

The temporal evolution of the signal can be lost. Bias correcting 2011–2100 in one block against 1971–2000 reproduces the change over the whole block well, but any decadal subset inside it may not. Their fix is to correct in shorter moving blocks — a decade at a time, each calibrated on the preceding one.

SDM assumes normality for temperature. Fine for daily mean T2M in most months; check it before trusting the tails in a strongly skewed season.

Separating bias adjustment from downscaling

Lange, S. (2019). Trend-preserving bias adjustment and statistical downscaling with ISIMIP3BASD (v1.0). Geosci. Model Dev. 12, 3055–3070. doi:10.5194/gmd-12-3055-2019 · CC BY 4.0
Lange2019_ISIMIP3BASD_GMD.pdf

Why it matters here

The production-grade answer to Maraun's objection, and the operational method behind ISIMIP3. Lange's opening move is the one we should copy: when the observations are finer than the model, bias adjustment is two distinct problems — correcting the bias at the model's own resolution, and statistically downscaling to the observation grid. Do them in that order, with different tools, and the inflation trap is avoided. The downscaling step is also stochastic, which is exactly what Maraun asks for.

Step 1 — trend-preserving bias adjustment

Rather than correcting the future directly, Lange generates pseudo future observations: the model's own quantile-by-quantile change signal transferred onto the historical observations. Quantile mapping then runs between the historical and pseudo-future observations, which is what makes the whole chain trend-preserving in all quantiles.

y = x + Δadditive(p),   Δadditive(p) = Qsimfut(p) − Qsimhist(p) Eqs. 1–2 — additive trend preservation, where x is a historical observation, p = Fobshist(x) its cumulative probability, and y the pseudo future observation. This is the branch used for tas, along with surface pressure and downwelling longwave radiation.
y = x · Δmult(p),   Δmult(p) = max(0.01, min(100, Qsimfut(p) / Qsimhist(p))) Eqs. 3–5 — multiplicative branch, for precipitation, wind speed and diurnal temperature range. The clip guards against a small historical quantile blowing up the ratio; ratios are set to 1 where Qsimhist(p) = 0.

Bias adjustment itself is parametric quantile mapping — the paper argues this is more robust in the extreme quantiles than nonparametric mapping — applied per variable, per grid cell, per calendar month. Bounded variables get suitable distributions (e.g. beta), and annual cycles of physical bounds are estimated with a 31-day running window.

Step 2 — stochastic statistical downscaling (MBCnSD)

Once each coarse cell's distribution is unbiased, downscaling is treated as one more bias adjustment problem: broadcast the coarse series to the K fine cells inside it, then adjust the multivariate distribution of those K time series jointly. The engine is Cannon's (2017) MBCn algorithm — repeated random rotations of the data followed by univariate nonparametric quantile mapping along each rotated axis — modified so that the coarse-scale mean is preserved.

KFine cells per coarse cell; each must nest exactly inside one
2°→1°Their worked example, giving K = 4
31 dRunning window for annual cycles of bounds

The grid requirement — read this before adopting the method

MBCnSD requires every fine cell to sit entirely inside one coarse cell. Our grids do not nest: CMIP6 at ~1° against ERA5-Land at 0.1° over 24–38°N, 30–38°E. Lange's own remedy is the fix — interpolate the simulation, before bias adjustment, onto an intermediate grid that is both compatible with the fine grid and close in resolution to the original coarse grid. That is an extra regridding step our pipeline does not have yet.

Caveats

Improvements are demonstrated by cross-validation, not against an independent future observation — the usual and unavoidable limit for any bias adjustment claim.

Trend preservation is “approximate”, and the multiplicative branch is deliberately abandoned where a large historical negative bias would produce unrealistic values.

Does subsampling help? The one paper that tests it

Reiter, P., Gutjahr, O., Schefczyk, L., Heinemann, G., Casper, M. (2018). Does applying quantile mapping to subsamples improve the bias correction of daily precipitation? International Journal of Climatology 38, 1623–1633. doi:10.1002/joc.5283
Reiter2018_QM_Subsamples_IJC.pdf

Why it matters here

Our whole design rests on estimating distributions per season rather than over the full year. Everyone stratifies, almost nobody argues for it, and this is the paper that actually varies the timescale and measures the result. It also turns out to contain the clearest published warning about the failure mode we hit.

The question, stated precisely

Quantile mapping corrects the whole distribution but not errors in the annual cycle, which is why it is usually applied to temporal subsamples. But subsampling shrinks the calibration sample. Does the sample-size loss cancel the benefit?

40Years of daily precipitation
10RCM hindcast runs
4QM methods, under cross-validation
4Timescales: none, semi-annual, seasonal, monthly

The answer

“Calibrating QM with subsamples instead of the calibration data as a whole resulted in a clear benefit.” Reiter et al. 2018, Discussion

So stratify. But the useful part is where the benefit stops. The gain was “more distinct for the corrections of the calibration data than for the corrections of the independent validation data”. In sample, finer was always better — “the finer the QM subsampling timescale was chosen the better was the performance”. Out of sample the improvement flattened, and the optimum depended on the method, landing somewhere between semi-annual and monthly.

The warning in Figure 4

At monthly resolution the more complex methods fitted the calibration extremes well. Applied to independent data, those same transfer functions corrected extremes “to unrealistically high values”.

Fine subsampling plus a flexible method equals overfitting, and it is invisible until you score out of sample. This is the same failure mode Gudmundsson et al. (2012) warn about for small calibration samples and Lehner et al. (2023) describe as non-parametric QM depending more on the calibration period — and it is why our own flexible methods retreat to their smoothest settings on a ten-year record.

What we took from it

  • Stratify — the benefit is real and demonstrated.
  • Season, not month, on a ten-year record. The paper shows the out-of-sample gain from going finer is small, while the overfitting risk is not.
  • Score out of sample or not at all. In-sample scores would have ranked the flexible methods first, which is exactly the trap.

Caveats

Precipitation, not temperature, and RCM hindcasts rather than a GCM. Precipitation is bounded at zero, intermittent and far more skewed, so the specific optimum does not transfer — only the shape of the trade-off does. They also had 40 years where we have 10, which pushes our own optimum coarser still.

Temperature tails are not Gaussian, and it matters by an order of magnitude

Ruff, T. W., Neelin, J. D. (2012). Long tails in regional surface temperature probability distributions with implications for extremes under global warming. Geophysical Research Letters 39, L04704. doi:10.1029/2011GL050610
Ruff2012_LongTails_GRL.pdf

Why it matters here

Our distribution-derived transform fits a normal distribution to each side. This paper is the evidence that the assumption fails in the tails, measured on station records rather than argued from theory. It is the reason the normality check belongs in the notebook as a measurement, and the reason the non-parametric transforms are worth carrying in the comparison at all.

What they did

29 stations with long records across several climate regimes, from the NCDC archive, and three variables at each: daily maximum, daily minimum and daily mean temperature. Distribution tails were examined against a fitted Gaussian, separately for DJF and JJA. The motivation came from earlier work showing that column water vapour and passive tropospheric tracers have longer-than-Gaussian, roughly exponential tails.

The finding

“non-Gaussian tails to be common in station measurements over a range of locations and climate zones” Ruff & Neelin 2012, Discussion and Conclusions

Departures go both ways. Some stations have longer-than-Gaussian, approximately exponential tails; others have shorter-than-Gaussian tails, which the authors note they cannot yet explain from the tracer-advection prototype and flag for future work. The two cases have opposite consequences for how threshold exceedances change under warming.

The number worth quoting

30%Exceedance probability under the observed exponential tail
0.85%The same threshold under a fitted Gaussian
×35The factor by which normality misstates it

That is the cost of assuming normality where the tail is not normal, for one station and one threshold. It is why the distribution-derived method should not be expected to carry P5 and P90 as well as it carries the median.

Caveats

Station point measurements, not gridded reanalysis, and 29 stations is a survey rather than a climatology — Perron and Sura (2013) is the global companion. None of the stations are in our domain.

Note also what the paper is about: the behaviour of genuine extremes and threshold exceedances. We report P5 and P90, which sit well inside the distribution, so the effect on our numbers is real but much smaller than these headline figures.

A global climatology of how non-Gaussian daily fields are

Perron, M., Sura, P. (2013). Climatology of Non-Gaussian Atmospheric Statistics. J. Climate 26, 1063–1083. doi:10.1175/JCLI-D-11-00504.1
Perron2013_NonGaussian_JClimate.pdf

Why it matters here

The reference our own skewness and kurtosis maps should be read against. It maps the same two moments, for the same variable (air temperature), split the same way (winter against summer), on 62 years of reanalysis — so it says whether what we measure over the EMME domain is ordinary or unusual.

What they did

62Years of daily NCEP–NCAR Reanalysis I
9Variables, including air temperature
2Seasons shown, DJF and JJA

Skewness and excess kurtosis — the third and fourth central moments, both zero for a normal distribution — computed at every grid point over the whole record. Only statistically significant values are plotted, and the moments are also zonally averaged to show the vertical structure.

The finding

“A common assumption in the earth sciences is the Gaussianity of data over time. However, several independent studies in the past few decades have shown this assumption to be mostly false.” Perron & Sura 2013, Abstract

The practical value for us is the method rather than the conclusion: compute the two moments per grid point, per season, map them, and treat departure from zero as the measurement. That is exactly what our notebook does, which makes the comparison direct.

Caveats

Reanalysis on a coarse grid, so it cannot speak to the 0.1° scale we work at, and the significance testing assumes independence between days that daily fields do not have.

What monthly adjustment costs, and why thirty years is the norm

Lehner, F., Nadeem, I., Formayer, H. (2023). Evaluating skills and issues of quantile-based bias adjustment for climate change scenarios. Advances in Statistical Climatology, Meteorology and Oceanography 9, 29–44. doi:10.5194/ascmo-9-29-2023 · open access
Lehner2023_QM_BiasAdjustment_ASCMO.pdf

Why it matters here

Two sentences in this paper support two separate choices of ours, and a third explains a result we did not expect. It is the most directly quotable reference for the seasonal window, for the ten-year caveat, and for why our flexible methods keep selecting their smoothest settings.

On sub-annual stratification, and on calibration length

“The daily data of each grid cell of the model are adjusted separately with the observations on a monthly basis. For the calibration data, a time period of 30 years is typical, since the statistical distribution of data of a shorter time period can be very noisy and a longer time period usually has pronounced climatological trends.” Lehner et al. 2023, Section 3

The first half is evidence that adjusting per grid cell per calendar month is established practice, which is what we do at season level. The second half is the cleanest published statement of our central limitation: we have ten years, not thirty, and the paper names precisely the consequence — a noisy distribution. It is also the argument for season over month, since season buys about 820 training days against a month's 300.

On why flexibility does not pay off on a short record

“non-parametric QM depends more on the calibration period than parametric QM” Lehner et al. 2023, Section 2

This predicts what we observe: QUANT selects very few lookup nodes and RQUANT selects wide smoothing windows, both retreating towards the smooth end of their grids. The paper also cites Switanek et al. (2017) for the view that extremes are corrected more robustly by a parametric approach, "as the return level of the most extreme event is somewhat random", and Themeßl et al. (2012) for the converse warning that parametric QM can introduce new biases because the true distribution is unknown and varies by region and season.

Caveats

An evaluation paper rather than a methods paper: it does not test the stratification choice against alternatives, and presents monthly adjustment as established practice rather than justifying it. For a direct test see Reiter et al. (2018), which is the one study that varies the subsampling timescale deliberately — and which is paywalled and therefore not in this folder.

Three things all five agree on

  • The core transform is one line. x̂ = Fobs−1(Fmod(x)). Everything else is a choice about how to estimate the two CDFs and what to do about the future.
  • Empirical CDFs beat fitted ones for pure bias correction (Gudmundsson 2012) — but Lange (2019) prefers parametric fits precisely because the tails of an empirical CDF are unstable, which is the same worry stated from the other side.
  • Bias stationarity is assumed by all of them and defended by none.

What this means for notebook 03

Our current baseline is the diagnostic layer under plain QM: five coarse-to-fine predictors × four window schemes × five percentiles, comparing distributions rather than paired days. The reading set says three things about where it goes next.

One. Comparing distributions instead of matched days is the right call (Maraun 2013) — there is no day-to-day correspondence to regress on.

Two. Plain QM across a ~10× resolution gap is inflation, not downscaling. Temperature's spatial coherence and our explicit Δz term soften this, and Maraun says so directly, but the point has to be stated in the write-up, not assumed away.

Three. Any step towards 2081–2100 needs a trend-preserving variant. QDM (additive form) is the cheapest upgrade; SDM's temperature recipe is the closest match to our variable; ISIMIP3BASD is the most complete, at the cost of an intermediate nesting grid.

A note on the 14-day window

A 14-day window holds 14 values, so its P5 and P90 are estimated from roughly one observation each tail. Gudmundsson's overfitting warning and Cannon's extrapolation problem both bite hardest there. State this caveat whenever short-window results are reported.
